The big idea

A sampling distribution is the distribution of a statistic — like the sample mean — computed from many, many random samples of the same size N taken from the same population. It is a theoretical distribution that describes how the statistic varies from sample to sample.

Three distributions, don't mix them up

  • ① Population: every score that could exist (top chart). Its mean is μ, its SD is σ.
  • ② Sample: one particular set of N scores drawn from the population (middle chart). Its mean is the statistic M.
  • ③ Sampling distribution: the histogram of that statistic across thousands of samples (bottom chart).

The Central Limit Theorem (what this proves)

  • The sampling distribution of the mean becomes approximately normal as N grows — even if the population is skewed or bimodal.
  • Its mean equals the population mean: mean of M ≈ μ.
  • Its standard deviation (the standard error) shrinks as N grows: SE = σ ⁄ √N.
  • So with a larger sample size, sample means cluster more tightly around μ — bigger samples give more precise estimates.

Things to try

  • Start with Uniform or Skewed populations, set N = 2, then draw 10,000 samples. Note the shape.
  • Raise N to 30+ and watch the sampling distribution become bell-shaped (the CLT in action).
  • Compare observed SD of the means to the theoretical σ ⁄ √N — they converge as replications grow.
  • Pick a skewed population and track the mean, then switch to median or variance to see how each statistic has its own sampling distribution.
  • Draw a custom population by dragging on the top chart.

Note: The bottom chart is based on a finite number of samples, so it approximates the true sampling distribution. Statistics are computed with divisor N (matching the original applet); the unbiased variance option uses N − 1.
